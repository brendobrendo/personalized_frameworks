# The Haunting of Searles Castle

The afternoon sun was sinking behind the Berkshire hills, casting long, sharp shadows through the imposing, pseudo-medieval stone gates of **Searles Castle in Great Barrington, Massachusetts.** I stood on the driveway alongside Mr. Abernathy, my retired high school history teacher.

Before he taught me the intricacies of the Gilded Age, Mr. Abernathy had been a student here in the late 1960s, back when the gargantuan French chateau-style mansion functioned as a strict private boarding school.

"Look at the masonry," Mr. Abernathy said, adjusting his glasses. "Edward Searles put his own name on everything after Mary died. But this castle belonged to Mary Hopkins Searles, America's wealthiest widow.

Mary was a curious woman. She delighted in being the talk of the town, but whenever someone's glance lingered too long, she would shield her face with her ebony silk fan. She craved attention, yet couldn't bear to be seen.

She poured her massive Central Pacific Railroad fortune into building this castle, intending it to bear her family name: *The Hopkins Estate.* But after her death, her young husband Edward inherited her fortune and the estate became known by his name.

And that wasn't the end of her humiliation.

Mary had an adopted son, Timothy Hopkins, who challenged Edward's inheritance in a bitter, highly publicized court battle. The trial dragged Mary's private life into the newspapers. Reporters portrayed her as an eccentric elderly widow who had married a man twenty years her junior. They scrutinized her fascination with Spiritualism, including accounts of séances held in her mansion.

Imagine that. A woman who had spent her life surrounding herself with grandeur, desperate to be admired, was now being remembered for the very things she would have preferred to conceal.

Even in death, she couldn't escape the public's gaze.

And in the autumn of 1970, we discovered just how much that mattered."

Mr. Abernathy led me down the gravel path toward the grand terrace overlooking the lower fields.

"It was my sophomore year. The school had admitted a new kid named Julian, a distant descendant of Edward Searles' family. Julian was arrogant, constantly bragging about his lineage and how his family owned the very halls we slept in.

It was late October, right around this time of year. A freezing fog had rolled off the Housatonic River, thick enough to swallow the lower boundary walls. Julian and I had broken curfew, sneaking out to the terrace to smoke cigarettes we'd smuggled from town.

Julian was leaning against the stone balustrade, laughing about how his ancestors had built the finest mansion in the Berkshires.

Suddenly, the night went silent.

The wind stopped. The crickets went quiet.

Then came a vibration.

At first, I thought it was my imagination. But the stone beneath my shoes was trembling. A low, rhythmic *chug-chug-chug* rose from somewhere beyond the terrace.

Julian laughed. 'Relax. It's just the train.'

But I knew the railroad tracks were down in town, nowhere near the castle lawn.

The vibration grew stronger. My cigarette slipped from my fingers. Then a deafening steam whistle tore through the fog, so loud that the windows behind us rattled in their frames.

A brilliant white light appeared in the mist.

It swept across the lawn like the headlight of an approaching locomotive. We heard the screech of iron wheels, the hiss of escaping steam, and the thunder of something enormous bearing down on us.

But there were no tracks. Nothing but grass.

Julian stopped laughing.

The light vanished, and the steam began to part.

A woman stood before us.

She was tall and regal, dressed in a sweeping black Victorian gown. Her pale hands were folded neatly at her waist. She seemed almost pleased to have an audience.

For a moment, neither of us moved.

Then Julian whispered, 'Who are you?'

The woman slowly raised her head.

I recognized her immediately. I'd seen her portrait hanging above the fireplace in the great hall.

It was Mary Hopkins.

Julian took a step forward, squinting into the darkness. He was trying to see her face.

The moment his gaze lingered, her entire body changed.

Her proud shoulders collapsed. She recoiled as though Julian had struck her. With a sharp *CRACK*, an ebony silk fan snapped open before her face.

For a fraction of a second, before the fan covered her completely, I saw what should have been her features.

There were none.

Only a smooth, white emptiness, like a piece of marble that a sculptor had never finished carving.

From behind the fan came a voice, trembling with fury and something that sounded almost like shame.

*'Why does my castle not bear the name Hopkins?'*

Julian stumbled backward.

The ground began shaking again. The whistle screamed through the night, louder than before.

Mary drifted toward Julian, her black gown trailing across the grass without disturbing a single blade.

He fell to his knees, covering his ears.

I grabbed his collar and tried to pull him away, but he wouldn't move. His eyes were fixed on the woman.

'Don't look at her!' I shouted.

I threw myself between them, blocking his view.

For one terrible moment, Mary turned toward me.

Her fan lowered slightly.

I squeezed my eyes shut.

The whistle rose to a deafening crescendo. Hot steam rushed across my face, carrying the smell of coal smoke and burning iron. Something heavy thundered past us, close enough that I felt the wind tear at my clothes.

Then everything stopped.

When I opened my eyes, the lawn was empty.

Julian was lying beside me, trembling uncontrollably.

We never spoke about what happened that night. Julian lost every ounce of his arrogance. He barely said a word for the rest of the semester and transferred out by Christmas.

But I couldn't forget what I'd seen.

For weeks afterward, I avoided the great hall. I couldn't bear to look at Mary's portrait.

And whenever a train passed through Great Barrington at night, I heard that same terrible whistle."

Mr. Abernathy fell silent.

We had reached the terrace. The evening air was growing cold, and the last sunlight had disappeared behind the hills.

"You know," he said, pointing toward the distant tree line, "regular passenger train service to Great Barrington ended on April 30, 1971. That's what the records say."

He stared across the empty lawn.

"After that, I never heard the whistle again."

I looked toward the castle. In the gathering darkness, its windows seemed almost black.

"Do you think Mary finally found peace?" I asked.

Mr. Abernathy didn't answer.

Instead, he reached into his coat pocket and removed a small, yellowed photograph.

It showed two boys standing on this very terrace, sometime in the late 1960s. One was unmistakably Mr. Abernathy. The other, I assumed, was Julian.

Behind them stood a woman in a black Victorian gown.

Her face was concealed by an ebony silk fan.

I stared at the photograph.

"Who took this?"

Mr. Abernathy shook his head.

"I don't know. I found it in my school trunk the morning after Julian left."

I looked closer.

Something about the photograph was wrong.

The woman wasn't standing behind the boys.

She was standing behind the photographer.

I could see her reflected in the glass doors of the terrace.

And her fan was lowered.

I looked up at Mr. Abernathy, but he was no longer looking at the photograph.

He was staring over my shoulder.

His face had gone white.

From somewhere deep within the castle came a faint, unmistakable sound.

*Chug.*

# The Haunting of Hopkins Hall
*A Berkshire Halloween Legend*

**October 27, 2026 · Brendan Smith**

## I. The Castle on Main Street

I grew up in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, in the heart of the Berkshires, what some people call Norman Rockwell country.

Every October, the maples along Main Street turn crimson, the Housatonic River runs dark beneath its bridges, and the whole valley smells of woodsmoke and fallen leaves.

But among the old churches and storefronts stands something rather unusual.

An enormous stone mansion, with towering chimneys, steep slate roofs, and windows that seem to watch the street below.

Everyone calls it **Searles Castle**.

Last October, I visited the castle with my old English teacher, Mr. Albright, who had attended boarding school there as a boy and now served as its caretaker.

He still wore the same corduroy jacket I remembered from school. But he seemed thinner, and he kept glancing over his shoulder as he led me through the entrance hall.

"Do you know whose castle this really is?" he asked.

## II. The Forgotten Widow

The castle was built in the 1880s by Mary Hopkins, one of the wealthiest widows in America.

Her first husband, Mark Hopkins, had helped finance the transcontinental railroad. After his death, Mary inherited an enormous fortune.

She returned to the Berkshires, where her family had deep roots, and commissioned a magnificent estate.

But Mary was an unusual woman.

According to old stories around town, she sometimes walked along Main Street holding a black fan across her face, as though this might prevent anyone from recognizing America's richest widow.

Yet she also seemed rather pleased by the attention her fortune attracted.

She could hardly bear to be stared at, Mr. Albright explained, but she was equally offended when people failed to notice her.

Then Mary married again.

Her second husband, Edward Francis Searles, was a talented designer who had worked for a prominent decorating firm in New York City. Considerably younger than Mary, he helped design and furnish her extraordinary home.

When Mary died in 1891, Edward inherited much of her fortune.

And eventually, her beloved estate became known as Searles Castle.

"Mary was a difficult woman to understand," Mr. Albright said. "I don't think she ever quite understood herself."

## III. The Midnight Whistle

When Mr. Albright was a boarding-school student, every boy knew the legend.

At night, Mary's ghost wandered the castle in a black Victorian gown, searching for Edward, who escaped through secret passages concealed within the walls.

Sometimes the students heard footsteps running behind the plaster. Other nights, a woman weeping.

And every Halloween, at midnight, a locomotive whistle echoed from the railroad tracks.

But no train could be seen.

The boys believed it was Mark Hopkins, returning on the railroad that had made his fortune, to help Mary find the man who had taken her legacy.

"Did Mark ever find Edward?" I asked.

Mr. Albright stopped walking.

"Something happened in 1971," he said.

## IV. The Memorandum

Mr. Albright was fourteen that year.

It was Halloween night, shortly before midnight.

Unable to sleep, he wandered into the castle library.

The room smelled of old leather and damp wood. As he passed the bookshelves, he heard a woman softly humming.

An elderly woman in a black gown stood beside the far window.

She held a black fan across her face.

When he looked toward her, she turned away.

But as he started toward the door, the humming stopped.

He glanced back.

She had lowered the fan and was watching him.

Then she raised it again.

Mr. Albright noticed a book lying open on a reading desk.

He approached it cautiously.

Tucked between its dusty pages was a folded sheet of yellowed paper.

It was a signed memorandum, written in an elegant, old-fashioned hand.

At the bottom was the signature:

**Mary F. S. Hopkins Searles.**

He recognized it immediately. The same signature appeared on a framed letter displayed in the castle's entrance hall.

The memorandum instructed that the estate should forever be known as **Hopkins Hall**, in honor of Mary's first husband.

Mr. Albright read the name aloud.

"Hopkins Hall."

Behind him, a floorboard creaked.

He turned.

Two figures stood in the library doorway.

A woman in a long black gown.

And a tall gentleman in an old-fashioned frock coat.

Their faces were hidden in shadow.

Then, far down in the valley, came the whistle of a train.

Somewhere inside the castle walls, a man screamed.

Mr. Albright fled the library.

## V. The Woman in the Window

After that night, the castle changed.

Or perhaps, Mr. Albright admitted, he had simply begun noticing things.

He would sometimes see Mary standing at the library window, her black fan raised.

When he looked directly at her, she disappeared.

But when he turned away, he could hear her humming again.

Once, he found the words **MARY WAS HERE** scratched into the library desk.

He rubbed his thumb across the letters.

The wood was freshly cut.

The next evening, the inscription appeared on another desk.

Then on a door.

Then on the inside cover of his schoolbook.

At first, Mr. Albright thought Mary was grateful.

But her behavior became stranger.

One evening, he entered the library and found every book on the shelves turned backward, its spine facing the wall.

All except one.

The book containing her memorandum.

He reached for it.

Behind him, a woman gasped.

He turned and saw Mary standing beside the fireplace, her fan trembling in her hand.

For a moment, she seemed almost eager for him to speak.

"Mrs. Hopkins?" he whispered.

She snapped the fan over her face.

The books fell from the shelves.

And Mary vanished.

Mr. Albright stopped going into the library.

But the inscriptions continued.

One appeared inside his locked trunk.

Another on the underside of his bed.

Then, during a weekend away from school, he opened his suitcase in a hotel room forty miles from Great Barrington.

Scratched into the leather lining were three familiar words.

**MARY WAS HERE.**

## VI. Mary Was Here

Mr. Albright fell silent.

We were standing in the library, beside the very window where he had first seen Mary.

"For more than fifty years," he whispered, "I've tried not to think about her."

"Then why tell me now?"

He stared toward the window.

"I thought perhaps she'd leave me alone."

He hesitated.

"Though I don't know why I thought that."

A faint humming came from somewhere behind the shelves.

Mr. Albright's eyes widened.

I turned toward the sound.

Nothing.

When I looked back, he was staring at the reading desk.

Across its polished surface, three words had appeared.

**MARY WAS HERE.**

Mr. Albright backed toward the door.

"Don't look at her," he whispered.

Then, almost immediately:

"No. Don't ignore her."

I left the castle without saying goodbye.

I haven't seen Mr. Albright since.

But yesterday, I received a letter.

There was no return address.

Inside was a single sheet of yellowed paper.

At the bottom was a signature I recognized from Mr. Albright's description.

**Mary F. S. Hopkins Searles.**

And above it, three words.

**MARY WAS HERE.**

I don't know why I'm telling you this story.

*Pause.*

But now you've heard it.

*Advance to the final slide.*

# Revision Notes

# The Haunting of Hopkins Hall — Story Development Notes

## 1. Criteria for Evaluating the Story

Use these questions to evaluate each revision:

### A. Horror and Suspense
- Is the story genuinely frightening, or merely mysterious?
- Does tension build progressively toward the ending?
- Does the audience feel increasingly vulnerable as the story unfolds?

### B. Clarity
- Can an audience hearing the story for the first time easily follow the characters, timeline, and central conflict?
- Are the supernatural rules understandable without excessive explanation?
- Does each major event have a clear cause and consequence?

### C. Immersion and Believability
- Does the setting feel like a real place the audience could visit?
- Do historical details, sensory descriptions, and character behavior make the events believable?
- Can the audience imagine themselves inside the castle?
- Does the story maintain enough ambiguity to make listeners question what actually happened?

### D. Nature and Power of the Supernatural
- How powerful is the haunting, and what are its limitations?
- Could the supernatural events be explained by madness, paranoia, unreliable memory, or coincidence?
- Who holds the power at different stages of the story: Mary, Edward, Mr. Albright, or the narrator?
- Can characters escape the haunting simply by leaving the castle, or does it follow them?
- Is the haunting a localized phenomenon, a psychological obsession, or an inescapable force capable of altering reality itself?
- How much should the audience understand about the supernatural rules, and how much should remain unexplained?

## 2. Character Development

### Mary Hopkins — From Sympathetic Victim to Unsettling Presence

Develop Mary's personality beyond her desire to reclaim her name and legacy.

- Initially, Mary should appear sympathetic: a woman whose wealth, home, and identity were appropriated after her death.
- As the story progresses, reveal an increasingly paranoid, obsessive, and emotionally unstable personality.
- Mary simultaneously **craves attention and recoils from it when she receives it**.
- Her behavior should have a nervous, unpredictable quality that makes the audience uncomfortable.
- Her contradictions should create tension: she desperately wants to be remembered, but may resent, distrust, or punish those who acknowledge her.
- By the end, the audience should question whether Mary's original grievance explains her behavior or merely provided an outlet for something more disturbing.

**Development question:** What specific actions could reveal Mary's paranoia and contradictory desires without explicitly describing her psychological state?

### Mr. Albright — The Unreliable Witness

Give Mr. Albright a more active role and a clearer motivation.

- Mr. Albright is now the **caretaker of Searles Castle**, in addition to being the narrator's former English teacher and a former boarding-school student.
- His lifelong connection to the castle gives him both credibility and a reason to remain near the haunting.
- He tells the narrator Mary's story because he believes that doing so might finally persuade Mary to leave him alone.
- His behavior should suggest that years of exposure to the haunting have made him anxious, paranoid, or psychologically unstable.
- The audience should question whether Mr. Albright is a reliable witness or whether his experiences could be explained by madness.
- His motivation should become apparent gradually rather than being revealed immediately.

**Development question:** Is Mr. Albright deliberately passing the haunting to the narrator, or is he so desperate for relief that he doesn't fully understand the consequences?

## 3. Three Narrative Layers and Their Horror Styles

The story contains three nested narratives, each with a distinct atmosphere and storytelling tradition.

### Layer 1: The Legend of Mary Hopkins
**Style: American Gothic folklore — Washington Irving's *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow***

- Emphasize the Gilded Age, the Berkshire landscape, local history, and inherited folklore.
- Establish Mary, Mark Hopkins, and Edward Searles as figures from a bygone era.
- Present the ghostly train whistle, hidden passages, and Mary's haunting as local legends passed down through generations.
- Give the supernatural events a timeless, folkloric quality.

### Layer 2: Mr. Albright's Account
**Style: Psychological and supernatural horror — Stephen King**

- Shift from distant folklore to a personal account of disturbing events.
- Emphasize Mr. Albright's psychological deterioration and uncertainty about what is real.
- Use recurring manifestations of **"MARY WAS HERE"** as an increasingly threatening motif.
- Reveal Mary's changing behavior through specific incidents rather than exposition.
- Blur the boundary between an external haunting and Mr. Albright's possible paranoia.

### Layer 3: The Narrator's Experience
**Style: Contemporary coastal/small-town supernatural mystery — *Widow's Bay***

- Bring the legend into the present through the narrator's personal connection to Great Barrington and Mr. Albright.
- Allow the unsettling events to move beyond Mr. Albright's account and intrude into the narrator's reality.
- Create uncertainty about whether the narrator has inherited the haunting or has been manipulated into believing it.
- Ultimately implicate the Toastmasters audience through the act of hearing the story.

**Overall progression:** Folklore → Psychological horror → Reality-breaking horror.

## 4. Setting and Historical Context

### Great Barrington and the Berkshires
- Establish Great Barrington as part of **Norman Rockwell country** to evoke an idyllic, nostalgic vision of small-town New England.
- Contrast this picturesque setting with the ominous presence of Searles Castle.
- Use the surrounding landscape and local history to strengthen the American folklore atmosphere.

### Edward Francis Searles
- Establish that Edward was a designer who worked for a firm in New York City.
- Explain his direct involvement in designing the castle, rather than portraying him merely as Mary's younger husband.
- Connect his architectural expertise to the castle's concealed passages and hidden spaces.
- Consider how his knowledge of the building could reinforce the legend that he continues to hide within its walls.

### The Ghostly Train
- Keep the supernatural train phenomenon minimal and ambiguous.
- People hear a locomotive whistle coming from the railroad tracks, but **no train can be seen**.
- Avoid descriptions of a visible ghost train, driver, or locomotive.
- Preserve the association between the whistle and Mark Hopkins without definitively proving that his ghost is responsible.
- Let the sound itself become a recurring omen.

## 5. Structural Revisions

### Opening: The Castle on Main Street
- Introduce the Norman Rockwell association to establish the idyllic New England setting.
- Establish Mr. Albright's present-day role as caretaker.
- Remove or replace the opening detail about the indecipherable words carved into the banister; it feels contrived and creates an unnecessary setup.
- Find a more natural way to introduce the castle's unsettling atmosphere.

### Historical Backstory: The Forgotten Widow
- Clarify Edward's profession, New York City background, and role in designing the castle.
- Develop Mary's personality and emotional attachment to the estate.
- Maintain a clear distinction between the historical figures and the supernatural folklore surrounding them.

### The Legend: The Midnight Engine
- Reduce the train's supernatural manifestation to an unexplained whistle.
- Preserve the uncertainty surrounding the source of the sound.
- Maintain the legend's simple central conflict: Mary searches for Edward, who has concealed her legacy.

### The 1971 Discovery: The Secret Passage
- Preserve the discovery of Mary's hidden will and the encounter with the shadowy figures.
- Consider whether Mary's behavior during this encounter can foreshadow her later instability.
- Ensure that the discovery changes the balance of power without requiring an elaborate explanation of supernatural rules.

### Part V: Replace "The Last Train"
**This section needs a complete redesign.**

- Keep Mr. Albright inside Searles Castle following the discovery of the will.
- Remove the scenes involving his parents and the family dining table.
- Focus instead on Mr. Albright observing changes in Mary's behavior.
- Show Mary's increasing obsession, paranoia, and contradictory relationship with attention.
- Introduce manifestations of "MARY WAS HERE" in ways that become progressively more disturbing.
- Develop a convincing incident through which Mr. Albright realizes Mary is no longer confined to the castle.
- Favor observable events, actions, and sensory details over explanations from Mr. Albright.

**Key development challenge:** How can the story demonstrate that Mary has escaped the castle without having a character simply announce it?

### Ending: The Haunting Spreads
- Gradually reveal that Mr. Albright is telling the story to escape Mary's attention.
- Preserve ambiguity about whether he is genuinely haunted, psychologically unstable, or deliberately manipulating the narrator.
- Show the haunting reaching the narrator through an event that cannot be easily dismissed.
- Maintain the final audience-directed twist: hearing Mary's story may allow her to follow the listeners.
- End with the inscription:

**MARY WAS HERE.**

## 6. Unresolved Creative Decisions

These questions should guide the next stage of development:

1. **What does Mary actually want?** Recognition, control, companionship, revenge, or something she herself cannot understand?
2. **Why does attention frighten Mary?** Is she ashamed, suspicious, afraid of being forgotten again, or fundamentally unable to accept satisfaction?
3. **What is the strongest evidence that Mr. Albright might be unreliable?** Can it be incorporated without undermining the supernatural ending?
4. **What event reveals that Mary is no longer confined to Searles Castle?** Can it be demonstrated visually rather than explained?
5. **What does telling the story accomplish?** Does it transfer Mary's attention, spread her influence, or merely make people susceptible to noticing her?
6. **How much supernatural power should Mary possess?** Is she a ghost attached to a place, a psychological contagion, or something capable of reshaping reality?
7. **What should the audience believe at the end?** That Mary is real, that Mr. Albright is mad, or that the distinction may no longer matter?
